# What the Data Reveals: Data Design seen with an Ethical Lens and through Structuralist Methods

## Defense of the User: Exposing Discriminatory Enforcement Through Data Transparency

Processing over 337,000 traffic stop records reveals that Black drivers are searched at 5.28% compared to White drivers at 3.16%, yet contraband discovery rates are 31.74% for Black drivers versus 34.85% for White drivers. Higher search rates aren't justified by evidence.
When normalized by population, Black drivers face 4 times the arrest rate overall and are 313% more likely to be arrested for Vehicle Regulatory Violations. By presenting this data in accessible visual formats without requiring FOIA requests, activists, legal advocates, policymakers, journalists, researchers, and community organizers can directly access the evidence they need to challenge unfair enforcement.

## Collectivization: Building Networks for Systematic Change

Legal professionals can access detailed case information while academics gain comparative data to advance research. Civil rights organizations can identify patterns to support class action litigation or policy campaigns, uniting around documented disparities of 313% arrest likelihood for Vehicle Regulatory Violations rather than relying on anecdotes.
Our analysis of police quotas opens discussion about systemic incentives driving discriminatory enforcement, despite their illegality in North Carolina. Journalists can probe departmental practices and budgetary structures. The Veil of Darkness analysis, while showing null results, demonstrates methodological transparency that builds trust and shows which analytical approaches work in different contexts.

## Materialization: Uncovering the Conditions of Data Absence

The North Carolina Bureau of Investigation offers traffic stop data only from 2020 to 2025. The years 2016 to 2020 are conspicuously absent, a gap corresponding to political transition in state leadership that suggests potential suppression or neglect during critical years.
The dataset ostensibly represents all of Wake County, yet only 10 stops occurred outside Raleigh township out of over 337,000 total. This raises questions about data collection practices: whose communities are being recorded and whose stops are missing from official records entirely.
Officer hash IDs are encrypted, preventing identification of patterns by specific officers or evaluation of whether certain officers disproportionately stop, search, or arrest Black drivers. These absences reflect power structures controlling what gets measured, who gets monitored, and which accountability mechanisms exist. D'Ignazio and Klein's Principle 7 demands we acknowledge both the work required to collect and analyze this data and the invisible labor of communities documenting their own experiences when official data fails them. 

## Increasing User Power in the Relationship: Enabling Community-Led Solutions

Interactive visualizations allow users to explore specific questions. A community organizer can examine search patterns in their neighborhood. A legal advocate can compare outcomes for different violation types. This self-directed analysis lets communities set their own research agendas.
Next steps should include community input on which additional variables to analyze and which visualizations are most useful. Partnerships with local organizations like Refund Raleigh and the Pretrial Reform Project could ensure the app evolves to meet community needs rather than academic interests.

## Power through Personal Change: Building Individual Capacity for Action

Someone who was searched during a traffic stop can contextualize their experience within broader patterns, transforming personal grievance into potential for collective action.
Legal advocates can challenge unlawful searches using contraband hit rate data showing White drivers have higher discovery rates despite lower search rates. Defense attorneys can use the 4x arrest disparity when arguing for dismissals or reduced charges.
Journalists can investigate without waiting months for FOIA responses. Seasonal spikes in summer months correlating with historical quota periods provide leads for reporting on current departmental practices. Students learn data analysis techniques through the methodological transparency in our code.
Unencrypted officer identifiers in future datasets would enable identification of repeat offenders, building capacity for targeted accountability.

## What's Next: Policy Proposals and Future Directions

### Immediate Data Collection Reforms

North Carolina must resume comprehensive, standardized statewide collection with public transparency. The missing 2016 to 2020 data should be recovered and published. Wake County must expand collection beyond Raleigh to suburban and rural townships.
Officer identification should be available in anonymized but trackable formats. Additional variables needed include officer race, years of service, precinct assignment, and geographic beat information.

### Legal and Policy Changes

Warrantless vehicle searches currently rely on probable cause evaluated through the reasonable person standard. This vague framework allows arbitrary enforcement. We recommend codifying specific, observable criteria that reduce officer discretion and racial bias.
Traffic stops should return to promoting traffic safety rather than investigating crime. Applying stops to curtail non-traffic-related crime produces systematic racial bias without corresponding public safety benefits.
Quotas must be abolished federally with strengthened enforcement. While North Carolina has ruled them illegal, seasonal patterns with summer spikes corresponding to historical bonus periods warrant investigation into current departmental incentive structures.

### Expanding This Work

Future iterations should incorporate qualitative data from community members, integrating personal narratives with statistics. Longitudinal analysis would reveal whether existing reforms have had meaningful impact since 2015.
Comparative analysis with other North Carolina counties would identify best practices and problem departments. Partnership with legal aid organizations could connect enforcement data with judicial proceedings and sentencing disparities. This framework could expand to create a national database enabling community organizers and researchers anywhere to access evidence for local advocacy.
