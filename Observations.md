
# Mugshots

It is remarkable how easy it is to find information about those arrested, but how concealed the same information is around arresting officers. One of the clearest examples of this is the mugshot. Arrestee's headshots in state matters are not technically in the *public domain*, but they are considered *public records*, which means that, depending on the state, authorities are free to disclose them at-will. In the digital-age, this frequently results in photos circulating within social media accounts and/or predatory [tabloid journalist accounts](https://www.ncsl.org/technology-and-communication/mug-shots-and-booking-photo-websites), freely accesible by both potential employers and colleagues. Since studies show that over 77% of employers [google prospective employees](https://scholarship.stu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1005&context=stlr) prior to coming to hiring decisions, this has grave implications for those who have ever experienced these encounters.

Many employers have a "zero tolerance" policy for any evidence of past arrest, and it is freely legal to fire or discriminate against employees based on mugshots, especially in states such as North Carolina where employment is [at-will](https://www.labor.nc.gov/workplace-rights/employee-rights-regarding-time-worked-and-wages-earned/employment-will). In the context of traffic stops, in which many arrests are either unlawful or based on arbitrary understandings of *probable cause*, those stopped may face ramifications that impact their financial status, reputation, and personal and professional relationships for the rest of their lives.
